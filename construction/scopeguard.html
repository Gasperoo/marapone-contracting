What buyout actually is

Fifteen letters. One afternoon. Days before award.

What comes back from the trades is prose — inclusions, exclusions and qualifications written by fifteen different estimators, each protecting their own number. Most GCs review it by hand, with a checklist, under time pressure.

The failure is never dramatic. It is one sentence in the concrete letter that puts winter heating on somebody else, on a job whose structure runs November to March. Nobody notices until the first cold morning, and by then the price is a change order rather than a negotiation.

Silence is the expensive part. The most costly item on a buyout is the one no letter mentions at all — so ScopeGuard reports absence as loudly as it reports a bad clause.

Inside the app

Every screen, no mock-ups.

Everything below is the shipping application running one real buyout: Lakeshore Yards, a 38-unit residential infill at 118 Jefferson Avenue in Toronto — $14.25M, twenty trade packages, fourteen proposals received, a full project manual and three addenda. Nothing is canned. The reader, the extractor and the cross-reference engine are the ones your own documents go through.

01 — Buyout dashboard

Where the money is leaking, ranked.

One screen answers the question you actually have: which trades to worry about, and how much. Exposure is shown per trade as a share of that trade's own quoted price — fire protection at 130% of its own quote is not a rounding error, it is a bid that priced a different job.

  • ▸A single buyout risk score with the reasoning behind it, not a colour-coded dot
  • ▸Trades still outstanding are listed separately and kept out of the exposure figure — an unreviewed trade is unmeasured risk, not zero risk
  • ▸"Fix before you award" pulls the highest-exposure findings to the top of the page
02 — The findings register

Cited on both sides, or it isn't a finding.

Every entry names the clause in your master documents that requires the work and the page and line in the sub's letter that deals with it. Below that: what to do about it, in a sentence you could paste into a letter. Ranked by severity, then by exposure.

  • ▸Every dollar figure states its basis — quantity, rate, and the share of that rate the requirement represents
  • ▸The range is an argument, not a hedge: the low end is buying it cooperatively now, the high end is buying it as a change order after mobilisation
  • ▸Mark any finding real or noise — that verdict persists and sharpens the next project
  • ▸Filter by trade, severity or category; export the whole register to CSV
03 — Requirement vs. proposal

Your spec on the left, their words on the right.

The review a good estimator does in their head, written down. Every master requirement for the trade, its treatment in the letter — included, excluded, qualified or never addressed — and where a treatment costs money, the sub's own sentence quoted verbatim beside it.

  • ▸Nothing paraphrased. The words you negotiate against are the words they wrote
  • ▸Requirements with no finding are shown as carried, so the clean part of a letter is visible too
  • ▸Exports as a per-trade scope report, or as revised scope language ready for the subcontract
04 — Prose into structure

A scope letter is not a data format. It becomes one.

Drag the proposals onto the window — PDF, Word, Excel, CSV or a saved email. Each is read, classified by trade and split into inclusions, exclusions, qualifications, unit rates and alternates, with the page and an extraction confidence on every line.

  • ▸Addenda acknowledged is tracked per letter — 3 of 3, or the gap that is about to cost you
  • ▸Revisions stack: import Rev 2 and the register updates rather than starting over
  • ▸Every extracted line is editable. The engine reads; you rule
05 — Clarification letters

One click turns findings into the letter you'd have written.

Every open finding on a trade, numbered, each with the clause that requires the work and the exact question to put to the sub. Paste their reply back in and ScopeGuard reads what it actually did — which items were withdrawn, which were priced, and which were answered with more prose.

  • ▸Draft for one trade, or for every trade with open findings at once
  • ▸Your standard scope language — blocking, firestopping, hoisting, winter, access panels, cutting and patching — is a library you edit once and it is used from then on
  • ▸Export the letters as PDF, and the final accepted scopes for the project file

06 — It learns your losses

Load your change-order history and the failures that actually cost you money get checked against every future letter. Alongside it: eighteen documented GTA dispute hotspots, and Ontario price movement by division — because a 30-day price on plumbing running 11.9% year-over-year is a buyout risk, not a formality.

07 — The evidence stays with the job

Every imported document is copied into the project's own folder. A finding that cites page 4 of a proposal is worth nothing once that proposal has moved off somebody's desktop — so it doesn't. Master package, addenda and every proposal, kept where the citations still resolve.

Why the numbers hold up

A range you can't audit is a range you won't use.

Nothing is invented

Exposure comes from a Toronto subcontract rate table applied to your project's own quantities. Severity comes from requirement criticality and your own dispute record. Confidence comes from the match score. MaraponeAI explains, prioritises and drafts — it never decides a number.

Every figure states its basis

Each finding prints the quantity, the rate, the share of that rate the requirement represents, and the stage multiplier behind its number. You can check the arithmetic in front of the sub, which is the only reason a number is any use in a negotiation.

Silence is a finding

The most expensive item on a buyout is the one no letter mentions. Absence is reported as loudly as a bad clause — and trades that have sent nothing at all are listed by name, with their budget, so the hole is visible rather than implied.

You overrule the engine

Every finding can be dismissed, every extracted line edited, every rate overridden. Dismissals and overrides persist and compound: the second project is measurably quieter than the first because you taught it what noise looks like on your jobs.

The model never decides a finding.

A tool that hands you 120 findings has to be right about them, because the first hallucinated exclusion you take to a subcontractor is the last time anyone in your office opens the app. So the cross-reference engine is deterministic: requirement matching, treatment classification, overlap and interface detection, exposure and severity all run on rules and your own data.

MaraponeAI explains, prioritises and drafts. Every figure it quotes traces back to a clause, a page and a rate. Ask it show me everything over $50k on mechanical and the instruction is parsed by rule and previewed before it changes anything on screen.

It runs as a deterministic reasoning engine out of the box. Drop a model file into the app's own folder and it adds free-form narrative — still entirely on-device, still with the wifi off.

100% local, no exceptions

No network calls. It works with the wifi off. Your subcontractors' pricing is the most commercially sensitive data in your office and it never leaves the machine — no account, no cloud, no telemetry, no per-seat licence phoning home.
