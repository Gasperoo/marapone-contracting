What leveling actually is

A day in Excel, and it ends in a hope.

Five bidders quote the same package and none of them quote the same scope. One carries crane hoisting; one excludes it; two never mention it; the fifth prices it as an alternate. Put those five numbers in a column and the column is meaningless.

On the demo package the low bid was $1,142,000. Levelled, that bidder is not the cheapest — awarding on the bid form alone would have cost $84,986, and $226,160 of scope sits outside the bid numbers entirely whoever you award to.

Silence is the expensive part. An exclusion is on the record and can be priced. Silence is what reaches site unresolved — so the two are levelled the same way and flagged differently.

The idea

The bid set prices its own gaps.

The interesting move is not "read a PDF". It is this: when Sub A excludes crane hoisting and Sub B carries it as a $15,000 alternate, Sub A's gap is worth $15,000. That is a real market price — for this project, this schedule, this site, from a contractor who wants the work. Not a number from a book. Not a percentage.

So the leveling pass looks for competitor evidence first and only falls back to the bundled Toronto rate table for elements nobody priced. Every adjustment shows which it used, because an estimator who cannot say where a number came from cannot use it in a negotiation.

Two rules keep the matrix honest, and both are enforced by tests

Silence on core trade scope is not a gap. A sub quoting "the HVAC work" for a lump sum is carrying the ductwork whether or not their letter enumerates it. Silence only counts where it means something — the universal items like crane and cleanup, and anything a competing bidder took a position on.

Hidden cost is the cost of the award, not the field. Summing every bidder's adjustments produces a figure several times larger than any cost anyone will incur. You award one subcontract, so the headline figure is the exposure on the bid you would actually award.

01 — Dashboard

One number: the cost of not levelling.

The headline is what awarding on the bid form alone would have cost you. Under it, the bid-versus-levelled bar for every bidder, ranked, with the position each one moved. Then a written review that says who to award to and what is still open against them.

  • ▸"Where the hidden money is" totals each scope element across every live bid — the field's exposure, not a cost on any one award
  • ▸Levelled award against your carry, with the variance stated as a percentage you can take to a meeting
  • ▸Ask it who should I award to? or what did nobody price? — parsed by rule, previewed before it changes anything
02 — The leveling matrix

Scope down the side, bidders across the top.

The day in Excel you were about to spend. Every cell says what that bidder did with that scope element — IN, EX, —, ALT, ALLOW, OTH or COND — with the adjustment underneath it.

  • ▸Filter to only the rows where the bidders disagree, which is where the argument is
  • ▸Click any cell to change that bidder's position or override the adjustment — a call you make by hand is marked and survives every re-run
  • ▸Exports to Excel as five sheets with live SUM formulas, so your overrides move the totals in the file too
03 — Prose into structure

A quote letter is not a data format. It becomes one.

Drag the quotes onto the window — PDF, Word, Excel, a saved email or a scan. Each is read and split into base price, inclusions, exclusions, qualifications, alternates, allowances, unit rates and commercial terms, with the page it came from and an extraction confidence on every line.

  • ▸Addenda acknowledged is tracked per bidder — 3 of 3, or the gap that is about to cost you
  • ▸Scanned quotes are recognised on the machine and flagged, not silently trusted
  • ▸A bidder who never sent anything can be entered by hand; every extracted line is editable
04 — The scope checklist

Decide what every bid is compared against.

The checklist is the basis of the whole exercise, so it is visible and editable rather than buried. 15 trade packages and 18 universal items ship with the app, seeded by trade and then extended from the bids themselves — if a bidder priced something the checklist didn't know about, it gets added.

  • ▸Switch an element out of scope and it stops generating findings — for work genuinely not in this package, not to quieten a finding you would rather not deal with
  • ▸Every element carries its CSI section, whether it is critical, its typical share of trade value, and the rate it is valued from
  • ▸Add your own elements — the ones your projects always argue about — and they persist
05 — Award

The answer, the arithmetic behind it, and the open items.

A recommendation with a status — award, award subject to clarification, or don't award yet — the levelled figure against your carry, and the high-severity items that are still open against the recommended bidder. Four documents come out of this screen.

  • ▸Award recommendation (PDF) — written to be read by a PM who was not in the meeting
  • ▸Clarification letters — one per bidder, every open item with the reason it is being asked. A vague letter gets a vague answer
  • ▸Subcontract scope paragraphs — written from the levelled position, so what leveling added back cannot be quietly re-excluded after signing
  • ▸Handoffs — JSON for the AI Estimator, CSV for ScopeGuard, a Procore-shaped bid tab, and raw JSON for your own tools

06 — Every finding is quoted, not summarised

The words you negotiate against are the words they wrote. Each finding names who carried the work and who didn't, quotes the evidence line from the document it came from, states the value and its basis, and gives you the question to put. Mark any of them real or noise; the verdict persists.

07 — It grades its own work

Record what you awarded and what it finally cost, and the app reports how well the levelled figure predicted the outcome against how well the bid form did. That number is the honest measure of whether this tool is earning its place — computed from your own history, on your own machine, and it is allowed to be unflattering.

Why the numbers hold up

The failure mode is not a crash. It is a plausible matrix that is wrong.

A levelled number an estimator cannot defend is worse than no number at all, because it is the one they will act on. Four things keep this honest.

Nothing is invented

Adjustments come from the bids first and the rate table second, and every one shows which. MaraponeAI explains, prioritises and drafts — it never decides a number, and it never finds a gap.

Every figure states its basis

Competitor price, their own alternate, your award history, the rate table, a percentage of trade value, or your own figure — ranked from strongest to weakest and printed on the adjustment. You can check the arithmetic in front of the sub.

Silence and exclusion are not the same thing

Both get levelled, because both cost money. They are flagged differently, because an exclusion is on the record and silence is what reaches site unresolved — and because silence on core trade scope is not a gap at all.

You overrule the engine

Change any coverage call, override any adjustment, edit any extracted line, take any element out of scope. Your calls are marked as yours and survive every re-run — the engine reads; you rule.

The model never decides a number.

MaraponeAI explains, prioritises and drafts. Every figure it quotes traces back to a line in a document or a row in the rate table. Type set crane to 18,000 on Northgate and the instruction is parsed by rule and previewed before it changes anything.

It runs as a deterministic reasoning engine out of the box. Drop a model file into the app's own folder and it adds free-form narrative — still entirely on-device, still with the wifi off.
